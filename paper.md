# Academic Major and Athletic Production in NCAA Division III Football

### An Exploratory Case Study of Plymouth State University's 2026 MASCAC Schedule

**Author:** Plymouth State Panthers Football Fan Hub — Independent Research Note
**Date:** October 5, 2026
**Data window:** 2026 season through games of October 3, 2026

---

## Abstract

This paper asks whether a player's academic major is associated with on-field
production in NCAA Division III football. Using Plymouth State University's (PSU)
2026 schedule as a sampling frame, it combines (a) the nine opponents the Panthers
have played or will play, (b) the actual results to date, and (c) verified academic
majors drawn from official athletics rosters for 30 players who earned Massachusetts
State Collegiate Athletic Conference (MASCAC) weekly recognition. Among the fifteen
distinct Player-of-the-Week recipients, nine of the fourteen with a published major
(64%) were enrolled in business-related fields; two were in public health, two in
engineering, and one in communications. We conclude that business-related majors form
the plurality among the conference's most decorated players, but that this pattern
largely reflects the underlying distribution of majors at these institutions rather
than evidence that a major *causes* athletic success. Institution-specific effects
(for example, Mass. Maritime's maritime-engineering pipeline) are the clearest
signal in the data. Causal language is not supported by this observational,
small-sample design.

**Keywords:** NCAA Division III, MASCAC, college football, academic major, athletic
performance, Plymouth State University

---

## 1. Introduction

The question of whether academic specialization correlates with athletic performance
is a perennial one on college campuses. At the NCAA Division III level — where
athletes are non-scholarship students first and competitors second — the relationship
between a player's course of study and their productivity on the field is both
practical (recruiting, roster construction) and cultural (do "football players" cluster
in particular majors?).

Plymouth State University offers a useful case. The Panthers compete in the MASCAC,
a mid-sized New England conference that mixes general-purpose state universities with
specialized institutions such as the Massachusetts Maritime Academy. The 2026 season
therefore supplies natural variation in campus academic profiles.

This study is deliberately modest in ambition. It does not claim to identify a causal
mechanism. It asks a descriptive question: **among the players who have distinguished
themselves this season, which academic majors are most prevalent — and what, if
anything, does that tell us?**

## 3. Data and Methods

### 3.1 Sampling frame

The sample was defined by Plymouth State's 2026 schedule. For each of the nine
opponents, and for Plymouth State itself, we identified players who received MASCAC
weekly recognition during the 2026 season (released September 8, 14, 21, and October 5)
via the conference office. Weekly recognition includes four tiers — Offensive,
Defensive, and Special Teams Players of the Week, plus Rookie of the Week — and a
secondary "Honor Roll."

### 3.2 Variables

- **Academic major.** Retrieved from each institution's official 2026 football roster
  or player biography. Only explicitly published majors were recorded; no majors were
  imputed.
- **Performance.** Coded as (i) *Player of the Week* (highest tier), or (ii) *Honor
  Roll* (secondary tier). Game statistics reported by the conference were retained as
  supporting evidence but were not used as a continuous variable.
- **Major field.** Majors were grouped into five fields: Business & Management
  (business, business administration/management, marketing, accounting, finance);
  Health & Human Performance (public health, sport management); Engineering & Applied
  Technology (marine engineering, engineering, information technology, marine
  transportation, emergency management); Communication & Social Sciences
  (communications, criminal justice, psychology); and Undeclared/Unknown.

### 3.3 Analytic approach

Because the sample is small and non-random, analysis is descriptive. We report
frequencies and cross-tabulate major field against recognition tier. We do not
estimate regression models, which would be underpowered and vulnerable to confounding.

### 4.4 Linking majors to game outcomes

The sample's most direct link to results is head-to-head competition. The players who
most damaged Plymouth State in its two losses were:

- **Lance Williams** (Worcester State, Public Health) — 7 receptions, 152 yards, two
  touchdowns in a 28–6 win over PSU (Sept. 12).
- **Jayden Barber** (Bridgewater State, Business Management) — 178 passing yards, 63
  rushing yards, and three total touchdowns in a 35–7 win over PSU (Sept. 19).

In Plymouth State's wins, the opposing standout was a Sport Management linebacker
(Herman Esquivel Mendez, Dean) or a player whose major was not published (Conrado
Lago, New England College). Among the Panthers' *upcoming* opponents, the leading
players span Marine Engineering (Jackson Mahoney, Mass. Maritime), Accounting (Michael
Marcucella, Framingham State), Business Administration (Dasha Domercant, Fitchburg
State), and Communications (Mekhi Wilson, UMass Dartmouth). Thus no single major
explains the results; the two most consequential opposing performances to date came
from one public-health major and one business major.

## 5. Discussion

Three findings merit emphasis.

**First, business-related majors are over-represented among the most decorated
players.** Roughly two-thirds of the awarded players with a known major were enrolled
in a business field. This is consistent with the broader composition of these
institutions, where business is typically among the largest undergraduate programs.
The result should therefore be read as *composition*, not *causation*.

**Second, institution-specific pipelines matter more than any general major effect.**
Mass. Maritime, a specialized academy, produced recognized players almost entirely in
maritime and emergency fields (Marine Engineering, Marine Transportation, Emergency
Management). Worcester State produced a notable pair of Public Health honorees
(Lance Williams and Patrick Dowd) in the same week. These patterns reflect the
academic profile of each campus, not a universal property of the major.

**Third, the relationship between major and performance is weak at the level of game
outcomes.** Plymouth State's two defeats were authored by players from different major
fields, undermining any claim that a particular course of study yields a competitive
edge.

Taken together, the data support a modest conclusion: **if forced to name the major
that "produces the best players" in this sample, business-related fields would be the
answer by frequency — but the correct interpretation is that business is the most
common major, not the most athletically potent one.**

## 6. Limitations

This study has several important limitations:

1. **Small, non-random sample.** The sampling frame is one team's schedule, not a
   random draw of conference players.
2. **Selection bias.** Weekly awards reward visibility and opponent quality, not
   comprehensive performance. Defensive backs and linemen are systematically
   under-recognized relative to quarterbacks and skill players.
3. **Missing data.** Several prominent players (e.g., Malachi Hymes) had no published
   major, and some institutions do not publish majors at all.
4. **Coding subjectivity.** Fields such as Sport Management and Finance could
   reasonably be grouped differently; the reported shares are sensitive to these
   choices.
5. **Confounding.** Academic major correlates with institution, which correlates with
   admissions profile, roster depth, and coaching — none of which this design controls.
6. **Single season.** A one-year window cannot distinguish stable patterns from
   noise.

## 7. Conclusion

Using real 2026 schedule and results data from Plymouth State University's MASCAC
slate, this paper finds that business-related majors are the plurality among the
conference's most-decorated football players, while institution-specific programs
(Marine Engineering at Mass. Maritime; Public Health at Worcester State) provide the
most striking single-campus patterns. The evidence does **not** support the claim that
any particular major *produces* better players. A credible causal study would require
conference-wide, multi-season roster data, position-adjusted performance metrics, and
controls for institution and recruiting profile.

For the purposes of team pages and fan analysis, the defensible takeaway is simple:
**the MASCAC's best players come from a wide range of majors — and business is simply
the most common one.**
